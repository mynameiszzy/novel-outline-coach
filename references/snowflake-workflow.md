# Snowflake outline workflow

Use the Snowflake Method as a progressive expansion process. Continue from the most developed reliable layer. Do not make the author repeat completed work, and do not force every project through every optional step.

## Expansion discipline

- Treat each stage as a useful artifact, not a mandatory interview checklist.
- When confirmed information is incomplete but sufficient for a coherent draft, fill non-blocking gaps with clearly labeled candidate assumptions.
- Preserve detail at its proper layer. Do not solve chapter or scene mechanics while the story promise and one-paragraph synopsis are still provisional, unless the user explicitly asks or the detail is a major structural hinge.
- Limit must-decide questions to those that would materially change the current artifact. Batch independent decisions and defer local texture.
- At a stage boundary, show the artifact, identify at most 3–5 high-impact uncertainties, and give a rough estimate of the remaining stages or interaction rounds.

## Stage 0: project frame

Capture only information that changes outline decisions:

- genre and intended reading experience;
- approximate length or format;
- viewpoint and tense when known;
- audience or content boundaries;
- existing premise, characters, ending, and non-negotiable elements;
- areas the author wants help deciding.

Exit when there is enough context to propose a story promise. Unknown fields may remain unknown.

## Stage 1: one-sentence story promise

Express the protagonist or focal group, destabilizing situation, central pursuit, opposition, and distinctive stakes in one economical sentence. Avoid names and lore density unless essential.

Test:

- Does it imply a protagonist who can make choices?
- Is there a change or conflict engine rather than only a setting?
- Does it distinguish this story from a generic premise?

If enough material exists, propose a candidate sentence immediately. Do not require every component to be individually confirmed first.

## Stage 2: one-paragraph synopsis

Expand into setup, major escalating turns or disasters, decisive climax, and ending. The outline should know the ending even when the finished story will conceal it from readers.

Do not mechanically assign one turn to each act. Ensure every major turn changes the protagonist's available options and makes the next phase necessary.

This is the first whole-story checkpoint. It may contain labeled candidate turns and ending assumptions. Prefer showing this imperfect whole over collecting detailed faction, character, or scene answers in advance.

## Stage 3: major character arcs

For each major character record:

- external goal;
- internal need or misbelief;
- motivation and stakes;
- capability and constraint;
- central conflict;
- decisive choices;
- change, refusal to change, or tragic realization;
- personal version of the story in one paragraph.

Characters may remain deliberately opaque to readers, but their outline logic must be known unless ambiguity is itself the design.

## Stage 4: extended synopsis

Expand each major beat into causal sequences. Track decisions, counter-actions, consequences, new information, and irreversible changes. Prefer a synopsis that explains why events follow, not only what happens.

At this checkpoint, use the three-act and narrative-line guidance when the story benefits from it.

## Stage 5: viewpoint versions

For multi-POV or ensemble stories, summarize the plot from each major viewpoint. Track what each character wants, believes, knows, hides, and misunderstands. Secondary viewpoints may need only a concise arc.

Skip this stage for a simple single-viewpoint project when it adds no value.

## Stage 6: detailed synopsis

Develop a complete beginning-to-end outline with enough detail to expose missing links, weak motivations, stalled middle sections, and unsupported revelations. Keep true chronology distinct from presentation order for nonlinear stories.

## Stage 7: character and world constraints

Add only details that affect choices or plot feasibility: relationships, resources, obligations, secrets, knowledge, power limits, institutions, costs, and exceptions. Avoid encyclopedic worldbuilding disconnected from the outline.

## Stage 8: chapter or scene inventory

Create stable IDs and record for each unit:

- viewpoint and time;
- immediate goal;
- obstacle or opposition;
- active narrative lines;
- new information;
- turn or decision;
- consequence and changed story state;
- setup or payoff links.

Every planned unit must change at least one meaningful state. Merge, remove, or redesign units that only repeat information or mood without intentional pacing value.

## Stage 9: scene briefs

Optionally expand complex scenes into entry condition, tactics, reversals, emotional movement, exit condition, and next-scene pressure. This remains outline work, not prose drafting.

## Stage 10: drafting readiness review

Run a standard logic audit. Confirm:

- the story promise survives expansion;
- major turns arise from character choice and opposition;
- the middle escalates rather than accumulates episodes;
- the climax resolves the principal external and internal questions;
- active lines and planted elements are paid off, intentionally deferred, or explicitly accepted as open.

Do not label an outline finished merely because all templates contain text. Finish when the causal and emotional path is usable for drafting.
