# Flexible three-act and multi-line structure

Use this as a structural lens layered onto the progressive outline. It is not a second mandatory workflow. The number of acts and lines may change when the story demands it.

## Three-act map

### Act I: establish and commit

- establish the ordinary or already-unstable state;
- introduce the principal relationships, rules, and unresolved pressure;
- trigger the disturbance;
- force or invite a consequential choice;
- cross a threshold that makes returning to the prior state costly or impossible.

### Act II: attempt, counterforce, and transformation

- let the protagonist pursue a strategy and learn its limits;
- let opponents and systems respond competently;
- intersect personal, relational, mystery, and world pressures;
- create a midpoint change in goal, knowledge, power, or commitment;
- escalate costs and narrow options;
- end with a failure, revelation, sacrifice, or choice that makes the final confrontation necessary.

### Act III: converge and pay off

- converge the lines needed for the central decision;
- make the protagonist act with the consequences of their arc;
- resolve the principal external conflict;
- reveal or confirm the thematic and relational result;
- pay off planted elements and state intentional open ends.

Do not judge acts by a fixed percentage alone. Diagnose their function, pressure, and causal transitions.

## Narrative-line registry

"Nine lines" means a rich multi-line design, not a requirement to create exactly nine lines. Use as few lines as the story can sustain, commonly 3–6 active lines for a long novel. Possible functions include:

- central external plot;
- relationship or romance;
- major supporting-character arc;
- mystery or information reveal;
- protagonist growth or decline;
- faction, history, or world conflict;
- family or social bond;
- thematic argument;
- deliberate relief or tonal counterpoint.

Treat relief as a pacing function unless it has its own question, progression, and payoff.

For each real line, record:

```text
ID and name
Central dramatic question
Owner or viewpoint
Opening state
Major turns
Intersections with other lines
Climax contribution
Payoff or intentional open state
Current status
```

Merge lines whose dramatic questions and turns substantially overlap. Remove or defer lines that do not affect the central plot, a character transformation, or the intended theme.

## Line-scene mapping

Tag each chapter or scene with the lines it changes. A line appearance counts only when its state changes, information advances, pressure increases, or a setup/payoff is performed. Mere mention is not progress.

Check for:

- long unexplained disappearances;
- too many new lines opening at once;
- secondary lines that never alter a major choice;
- lines converging by coincidence rather than causality;
- a climax overloaded with unrelated resolutions;
- emotional or mystery lines resolving before their promised pressure peaks.

## Nonlinear narration

Plan two separate structures:

1. **True chronology** — what actually happened, in causal order.
2. **Reveal order** — what each viewpoint and the reader learns, and when.

For every withheld fact, record who knows it, why it is withheld, what clue supports it, the reader's likely interpretation, and the payoff. A surprise should reinterpret available evidence rather than depend only on information introduced at the reveal.

## Scene necessity test

A scene should intentionally perform at least one of these functions:

- change the practical situation;
- advance or frustrate a goal;
- reveal consequential information;
- alter a relationship;
- plant or pay off an element;
- force a choice;
- change emotional pressure or pacing in a way the surrounding structure needs.

When scenes overlap, offer three repair directions: merge them, remove one, or change the later scene's outcome and function.
